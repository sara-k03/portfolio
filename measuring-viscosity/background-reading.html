1. What is mean-squared displacement (MSD) and the diffusion coefficient?

Source: Random Walks in Biology by Howard C. Berg

Diffusion is the random migration of molecules or small particles arising from motion due to thermal energy.

A particle at absolute temperature T has, on average, a kinetic energy associated with movement along each axis of $kT/2$

  • $k$ → Boltzmann constant

A particle of mass m and velocity $v_x$ on the x axis has a kinetic energy of $mv_x^2/2$. The quantity fluctuates so we usually want to denote this as an average over time/many similar particles: $\langle mv_x^2/2 \rangle$.

$$\langle mv_x^2/2 \rangle = \dfrac{kT}{2}$$

$$\langle v_x^2 \rangle = \dfrac{kT}{m}$$

$$\langle v_x^2 \rangle^{1/2} = \left(\dfrac{kT}{m}\right)^{1/2}$$

We can use this to calculate the instantaneous speed of a small particle. Applying this to a molecule of the protein lysozyme: the velocity ends up being really fast. But since the protein is in an aqueous medium, it doesn't go very far before it bumps into water molecules.

Instead it wanders around and executes a random walk.

Simple diffusion is when several of these particles are initially confined in a small region of space and then spread out in all directions.

Diffusion with drift is when an external force is applied and the particles spread out in a certain direction.

1D Random Walk:

Consider the following rules:

  1. Each particle steps to the right or left once every $\tau$ seconds, moving at velocity $\pm v_x$ a distance $\delta = \pm v_x\tau$. Assume $\delta$ and $\tau$ are constants.
  2. The probability of going right at each step is $\frac{1}{2}$ (likewise for going left). Because of the interactions with the molecules of water, each successive step is statistically independent.
  3. Each particle moves independently of all the other particles and they do not interact with each other. This is actually a reasonable assumption if the suspension of the particles is diluted enough.

Let $x_i(n) = x_i(n-1) \pm \delta$ where $x_i(n)$ is the position of the ith particle after the nth step differs from its position after the (n-1)th step by $\pm \delta$.

Summing all the particles over i and dividing by N:

$$\langle x(n) \rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i(n).$$

Expressing this in terms of $x_i(n-1)$, we get:

$$ \begin{aligned} \langle x(n) \rangle &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[x_i(n-1) \pm \delta\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i(n-1) = \langle x(n-1) \rangle \end{aligned} $$

Since $\langle x(n) \rangle$ is equal to $\langle x(n-1) \rangle$, the mean position of the particles doesn't change from step to step. Since all the particles start at the origin, the mean position remains at the origin. This tells us the spreading of particles is symmetrical about the origin.

To determine how much the particles spread, we use the root-mean-square displacement $\langle x^2(n) \rangle^{1/2}$. Averaging the square of the displacement ensures that the result can't be 0.

Following the same steps as above:

$$x_i^2(n) = x_i^2(n-1) \pm 2\delta x_i(n-1) + \delta^2.$$

$$ \begin{aligned} \langle x^2(n) \rangle &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2(n), \\ \langle x^2(n) \rangle &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[x_i^2(n-1) \pm 2\delta x_i(n-1) + \delta^2\right] \end{aligned} $$

The $x_i$ term becomes 0.

$$\langle x^2(0) \rangle = 0, \ \langle x^2(1) \rangle = \delta^2, \ \langle x^2(2) \rangle = 2\delta^2, \ \ldots, \ \langle x^2(n) \rangle = n\delta^2$$

Thus, the MSD increases with the step number n and the root MSD increases with sqrt of n.

According to rule 1, $t = n\tau$. So n is proportional to t. This means that MSD is proportional to t and the root MSD is proportional to sqrt t.

$$\langle x^2(t) \rangle = \left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\delta^2 = \left(\frac{\delta^2}{\tau}\right)t$$

For convenience, we now define a diffusion coefficient, $D = \dfrac{\delta^2}{2\tau}$.

(The factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ has a separate set of reasoning that is covered in a different chapter of Random Walks.)

This gives us

$$\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt \quad \text{and} \quad \langle x^2 \rangle^{1/2} = (2Dt)^{1/2}$$

D characterizes the migration of particles of a certain kind in a given medium at a given temperature.

There is also no such thing as diffusion velocity.

2D and 3D Random Walks:

The three rules outlined above apply in each dimension. We also assert that the motions in the x, y and z directions are statistically independent. If $\langle x^2 \rangle = 2Dt$, then $\langle y^2 \rangle = 2Dt$ and $\langle z^2 \rangle = 2Dt$.

Since $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, $\langle r^2 \rangle = 4Dt$ in two dimensions.

Since $r^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, $\langle r^2 \rangle = 6Dt$ in three dimensions.

One interesting feature of a random walk (that is elaborated on in later chapters) is that a particle tends to explore one region thoroughly before it moves on to another region. In other words, the particles don't tend to move towards regions of space that they have not occupied before.

2. How are diffusion and viscosity connected?

Source: Dongdong Jia; Jonathan Hamilton; Lenu M. Zaman; Anura Goonewardene, “The time, size, viscosity, and temperature dependence of the Brownian motion of polystyrene microspheres” Am. J. Phys. 75, 111–115 (2007)

$$\langle r^2 \rangle = \dfrac{2kT}{3\pi\eta a}t.$$

This is Equation 18 from the paper, which describes MSD in two dimensions. From the prior reading, we know that $\langle r^2 \rangle = 4Dt$.

To define the variables:

  • $t$ = time
  • $T$ = temperature
  • $k$ = Boltzmann constant
  • $a$ = particle radius
  • $\eta$ = viscosity
  • $r$ = displacement

The goal of this project is to solve for viscosity.

$$ \begin{aligned} \frac{2kTt}{3\pi\eta a} &= 4Dt \\ \eta &= \frac{2kT}{3(4)\pi a D} \\ \eta &= \frac{kT}{6\pi a D} \end{aligned} $$

(My algebra)

Therefore

$$\eta = \dfrac{kT}{6\pi a D}$$

k, T, π, and a are all standardized, so to measure the viscosity, we just need to measure D. D can be measured by plotting the MSD against time and finding the slope.
